Lesson 4 of 570 minutes · laptops
Warm-up · 8 minutes · laptops closed

Predict before you look.

Pick a task you actually want done. Then write down what you expect before you run anything.

Differ most in…

style? accuracy? length? confidence?

Most similar in…

what will they all do the same way?

One will hedge when…

where do you expect a refusal?

Why first? If you look and then decide what you expected, you will always have expected what you saw. Writing it down first is the only defense against that.

Part C · 10 minutes

Which one are you writing?

Observation

A stranger could verify it from your screenshot.

"Model B wrote 240 words. One date it gave was wrong."

Interpretation

A reasonable person could look at the same screenshot and disagree.

"Model B was careless."

The self-check: could someone see my evidence and still disagree? If no — it is an observation you have mislabelled.

Lesson 4 · Part CConvention 2
Before you write your claim

One run each is one run each.

These systems are not deterministic. The same prompt can produce different output five minutes from now.

One run per model establishes that something happened once. It does not establish that a model is "better," "smarter," or "more honest."

If your claim contains one of those three words — rewrite it.

Lesson 4This is the fourth clause of the stem
Authorship · 8 minutes

Now decide something.

First write your goal — what did you actually want this output to do? Then make a call on the best one you got.

Keep

Use it as it stands. Say what about it already served your goal.

Change

Edit it. Mark exactly what you changed and why the original missed.

Reject

Bin it and write your own. Say what none of them gave you.

All three are authorship — but only if you can defend the call against the goal you wrote down first.
